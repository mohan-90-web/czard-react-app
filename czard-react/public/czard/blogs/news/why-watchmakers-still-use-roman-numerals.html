Why Watchmakers Still Use Roman Numerals

An Ancient System on a Modern Wrist

Roman numerals are, by any practical measure, an outdated way of writing numbers. The world does its counting in Arabic numerals, has for centuries, and no one reaches for I, V, and X to do arithmetic. And yet, on the dial of a watch, those same ancient characters remain not only common but quietly prestigious. A watch with Roman numerals reads as classical, formal, considered. Why does a system the rest of the world set aside still feel so at home on a timepiece?

The answer is a mix of history, visual character, and the particular way watches carry tradition forward. Roman numerals survive on dials not because they are useful for counting, but because of what they have come to represent, and because, on the small canvas of a dial, they simply look right.

A Link to the Origins of Timekeeping

Roman numerals appear on watches because they were there at the very beginning of mechanical timekeeping. The first mechanical clocks were built in Europe from around the thirteenth century, at a time when Roman numerals were still in everyday use and Arabic numerals had not yet become common in the West. Many of those early clocks were mounted on churches and public buildings, where Latin was the formal language. Roman numerals were simply the natural, expected way to mark the hours.

So when Arabic numerals later became standard for everything else, clocks and watches had already established their own visual tradition. The dial became one of the few places where Roman numerals never left. To use them today is to keep a direct visual link to the origins of the craft, and that continuity is part of why they carry the weight they do. A Roman-numeral dial does not just tell the time. It points, quietly, to the long history of telling time.

The Curious Case of IIII

There is one detail on Roman-numeral dials that rewards a closer look, and once seen, cannot be unseen. On the great majority of watches and clocks, the four is not written IV, as correct notation would have it, but IIII, four strokes in a row. This is not a mistake. It is one of the oldest and most consistent conventions in watchmaking, and it has a story of its own.

There is no single proven reason, and honest sources admit as much, but a few explanations recur. In the earliest Roman usage, four was often written additively as IIII rather than IV, so the dial may simply preserve an older form. The most widely accepted explanation, though, is visual balance: on a dial, IIII gives the left side a visual weight that mirrors the heavy VIII on the right, so the numerals feel symmetrical around the face. A slender IV would leave that side looking light by comparison. Other accounts point to older mould-and-casting practicalities, and a long-repeated tale about avoiding the letters that began the Latin name of the god Jupiter. What is certain is that the IIII endures for reasons of tradition and balance, not error, and its persistence is a small monument to how seriously watchmaking takes its own conventions.

Why They Simply Look Right

Beyond history, Roman numerals earn their place on the dial through visual character. They bring a sense of formality and permanence that Arabic numerals rarely match. Their upright strokes and even spacing suit the radial arrangement of a dial, and their weight gives a face a classical, architectural quality. A Roman numeral looks less like information and more like design, which on a watch is often exactly the point.

They are not right for every watch. A tool watch or a sporting design usually calls for the plain, immediate legibility of Arabic numerals or simple markers. But for a dress watch, or any watch reaching for a sense of tradition and refinement, Roman numerals speak a language nothing else quite replicates. The choice to use them is a choice about character, a signal of the register a watch is written in.

Tradition Kept, Not Copied

The survival of Roman numerals says something about watchmaking itself. This is a craft that treats its conventions as worth preserving, not because they are the most efficient choice, but because they connect the present to a long and admired past. A Roman-numeral dial is a small act of continuity, a decision to carry something forward rather than replace it for the sake of the new.

This is the difference between tradition and imitation. Using Roman numerals thoughtlessly, because they look expensive, produces a hollow result. Using them with an understanding of why they belong, of the history they carry and the balance they bring, produces a dial that feels genuinely rooted. The numerals are the same either way. What differs is whether the maker understood what they were keeping.

A Detail That Carries History

That is the quiet lesson of Roman numerals on a dial. A choice that looks purely decorative turns out to carry centuries of continuity, a convention of visual balance refined over generations, and a whole register of formality and permanence, all in a handful of ancient strokes. It is a reminder that on a well-considered watch, even the way the hours are written is a decision with meaning behind it.

This is the attention CZARD brings to the elements of a dial: an understanding that every choice, including how time itself is marked, carries character and history worth respecting. When a detail is used, it is used because its meaning is understood, not because it looks the part. A watch made this way rewards the same closer look that Roman numerals invite, the discovery that nothing on it is arbitrary.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do watches use Roman numerals?

Roman numerals were in common use when the first mechanical clocks were built, so they became the traditional way to mark the hours and never left the dial. Today they are chosen for the classical, formal character they bring, and for their direct visual link to the origins of timekeeping.

Why do watch dials use IIII instead of IV for four?

There is no single proven reason, but the most widely accepted explanation is visual balance: IIII gives the left of the dial a weight that mirrors the VIII on the right. Other explanations include an older additive form of the numeral and historical casting practicalities. It is a deliberate convention, not an error.

Are Roman numerals just decorative on a watch?

They are functional, they mark the hours, but they are also chosen for character. Roman numerals bring formality and a sense of tradition, which is why they suit dress and classical watches more than sporting or tool designs.

Do all watches use IIII rather than IV?

Most do, but not all. A few makers deliberately use the correct IV, and some famous clocks, such as Big Ben, use IV as well. The IIII remains the dominant convention in watchmaking, largely for tradition and balance.

When should a watch use Roman numerals instead of Arabic ones?

Roman numerals suit watches reaching for formality, tradition, or a classical character, such as dress watches. Arabic numerals or plain markers usually suit tool and sporting watches, where immediate, unambiguous legibility matters most.
